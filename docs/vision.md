# vision.md

## Orange Coauthor

> Cold-emailing 40 professors doesn't work. Post what you want to research and it lands with the faculty already working on it, with the paper, the grant, and the reason it's a fit.

---

## The problem

Undergraduate research at a large university is gated by information, not by ability.

A student who wants to do research has to guess. They browse department pages, find names without context, and send cold emails that read identically to forty others. Most get no reply. The ones who succeed usually had an advantage unrelated to merit — a professor they already knew, or someone in their life who explained how academia works.

Faculty experience the mirror image. They receive a stream of generic emails from students who clearly haven't read their work, with no way to tell which are a real fit. Evaluating costs more than ignoring, so most get ignored.

Both sides want the same thing. Neither has the information to find the other.

That information already exists — scattered across publication records, grant databases, and portals no undergraduate has heard of.

---

## The vision

**Make the research landscape of a university legible, so finding the right collaborator takes a sentence instead of a semester.**

A student should describe what they're curious about in plain language and immediately see where that curiosity sits in their university's real research activity: which themes it touches, who works there now, what they've published, what they're funded to do.

A professor should see, without effort, which students are genuinely aligned with their work.

Long term, this is the front door to undergraduate research at Syracuse — the thing a student opens in their first year instead of their last.

---

## Who it's for

**Students** who want to do research and don't know where to start. Undergraduates primarily; incoming graduate students hunting for an advisor have the same problem.

**Faculty** who want better-matched students and less undifferentiated email.

**Advisors and departments**, secondarily, who have no map of who works on what across college boundaries.

---

## What it is

A matchmaking layer built on the university's own public research record.

**Student to faculty.** Describe an interest; get ranked faculty with the specific paper and funded project that justify each match.

**Student to research group.** Research happens in groups, not with lone professors. Co-authorship patterns reveal those groups, so a match can return a whole lab rather than one name.

**Student to student.** Several students posting adjacent ideas is a team forming. A formed team with a scoped idea is far more likely to get a yes than a single student cold-emailing.

**The theme layer is the heart of it.** Rather than matching student text directly against faculty text, the system first maps the university's entire research output into named themes, then places both students and faculty into that shared space. This makes every match explainable, makes the landscape browsable for students who don't yet know what they want, and produces the defining visual: a map of what this university actually researches.

---

## Principles

**Show evidence, not inference.** The system can see that a professor publishes in an area and holds a grant running through next year. It cannot see that they want a student. So it shows evidence and lets people draw conclusions — unless a professor has explicitly said they're open, in which case that's stated as fact. Never manufacture someone's intent.

**Every match explains itself.** A ranked list with no reasoning is a black box, and black boxes don't get trusted with a decision as consequential as who you spend a year working with.

**Currency over volume.** A professor who wrote one tangential paper a decade ago is not a match. Recency is weighted in theme placement, in group detection, and in ranking.

**Start populated, never empty.** The faculty side is built from public research output before anyone signs up, so the first student to use it gets real matches. An empty two-sided marketplace is a dead one.

**Consent before connection.** The system proposes; people confirm. It never announces someone onto a team and never contacts anyone on a user's behalf.

---

## What this is not

Not a job board. Not a messaging platform. Not a replacement for advising. Not a ranking of professors — it measures topical fit and public activity, never quality of person.

---

## Why this one

It solves a problem both sides of campus genuinely have. It runs on data that exists and that nobody thinks to use. The hard part — turning a scattered institutional record into a navigable map — is visible to anyone watching. And it works on day one for the first user, because the faculty side was populated before anyone arrived.
